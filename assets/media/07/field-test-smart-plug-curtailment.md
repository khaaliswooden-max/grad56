# Field Test 07: A smart plug as a curtailment rule (window air conditioner, peak hour)

**FILM IN SEASON.** This test is honest only on a hot afternoon during the summer peak. Film it in June, July, or August 2027, on a day in the upper 80s or 90s °F that is **not** under a heat advisory. Do not film it in December with a heater or a fan standing in. Until then the module page shows no video; the slot stays hidden.

## Safety and rules (read before filming)

- **Plug-in meters only.** The smart plug goes in the wall; the air conditioner's own cord goes in the smart plug. Nothing is opened: not the air conditioner, not the plug, not the outlet. Not on camera, not off.
- **Check the ratings first.** Read the air conditioner's label for amps. Use a smart plug rated for 15 A that its maker lists as suitable for air conditioners or motor loads. If the air conditioner draws more than 12 A, or has a 20 A or 240 V plug, don't run this test with it.
- **Heat is the hazard.** No one elderly, ill, pregnant, or very young in the room, and no pets shut in it. Not on a heat-advisory or excessive-heat-warning day. Set a stop line before you start: if the room reaches 85 °F, or anyone feels unwell, switch the air conditioner back on and end the test. Say the stop line on camera.
- **One switch-off, one switch-on.** Follow the air conditioner's manual about restarting; many say to wait several minutes after it stops before starting it again. Don't cycle it on and off.
- The on-screen line **"Plug-in meters only."** appears in shot 1 and stays as a lower-third whenever the plug is in frame.
- Name the smart plug, the air conditioner, the thermometer, and the day on screen and in the narration. Report readings exactly as measured. One room, one unit, one afternoon: never present it as a national figure.

## Readings to log (fill in on the day)

| | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Date; outdoor temperature at the start and its source | |
| Location ("back bedroom, door closed") and rough room size | |
| Smart plug make and model | |
| Air conditioner make, model, BTU, label amps | |
| Thermometer make and model | |
| Peak hour chosen, and where that came from | |
| Stop line (room temperature at which the test ends) | |
| **Baseline hour** (the hour before the peak hour, air conditioner running): watt-hours used; average watts; room temperature at start and end | |
| **Curtailed hour** (the peak hour, plug off): watt-hours used; room temperature every 15 minutes (0, 15, 30, 45, 60) | |
| Watt-hours saved = baseline hour − curtailed hour | |
| Degrees gained = room temperature at 60 min − at 0 min | |
| **Recovery hour** (plug back on): watt-hours used; minutes to return to the starting temperature | |
| Net saved over the three hours = (2 × baseline hour) − (curtailed hour + recovery hour) | |

The recovery hour matters. An air conditioner that was off for an hour works harder afterward, so some of the saving comes back as extra use later. Report the net figure, whatever it is. That rebound is real on the grid too.

## Narration script

Read by Khaalis in his own voice, or rendered in Kokoro am_michael at speed 1.0 (then the video also carries "Narration: AI voice"). Brackets are filled in from the log; read numbers as words.

> This is a Grid and Grain Field Test. Plug-in meters only.
>
> It's [day of week], [month day], in [town], and it's [outdoor temperature] degrees outside. That's hot, but there's no heat advisory today, and nobody's in this room but me.
>
> Here's the setup. A window air conditioner, a [AC make and model]. A smart plug that logs energy, a [plug make and model], rated for fifteen amps. And a thermometer on the table. The air conditioner plugs into the smart plug, and the smart plug goes in the wall. Nothing gets opened.
>
> Right now the unit is drawing [watts] watts and the room is [temperature] degrees.
>
> This is today's demand on [grid operator], from the Energy Information Administration's hourly grid monitor. The top of the curve is [peak hour]. That's the hour the whole system is sized for.
>
> So here's my rule. For that one hour, the plug switches the air conditioner off. That's a curtailment rule, the same idea a utility writes into a contract with a big customer, just a lot smaller.
>
> Before it starts, my stop line: if this room hits eighty-five degrees, or I don't feel right, the air goes back on and the test is over.
>
> Off.
>
> Fifteen minutes: [temperature]. Thirty: [temperature]. Forty-five: [temperature]. One hour: [temperature].
>
> Back on. Look at the watts: [watts]. It's working harder now to catch up.
>
> Here are both sides of the bargain. In the peak hour I saved [watt-hours saved] watt-hours, and the room gained [degrees gained] degrees. Then in the hour after, the unit used [extra watt-hours] more than usual pulling the room back down. Net, over the three hours, I saved [net watt-hours].
>
> That's what this plug read, in this room, on this afternoon. Yours will be different.
>
> Here's why it matters. When a large customer tells your county it will be flexible, it's promising this same trade at the scale of a town: how many megawatts it drops, for how many hours, and what it gives up to do it. Ask for those numbers in writing. And ask how it will cut back: by using less, by running a battery, or by starting diesel generators. Those are three different neighbors.
>
> Plug-in meters only. And never run this test in a room with someone who can't take the heat.
>
> The whole module is free at getruralamericadone.com. Know the wires. Set the terms.
