## Safety and rules (read before filming)

- **Phone app only. Nothing is opened or touched.** Don't touch, open, lean on, or reach into the outdoor unit. Keep hands, the phone, the tape measure, and any cord away from the fan and the fins. Don't remove a panel or a grille, on camera or off.
- Turn the unit on and off **at the thermostat indoors**, never at the disconnect box or the breaker.
- Measure **only on your own property** (or with the owner standing there). Don't take readings at a utility substation fence or anyone's business.
- Pick a still day. Wind across the phone's microphone ruins the reading; if the leaves are moving, wait. No rain.
- The on-screen line **"Phone app only. Nothing opened or touched."** appears in shot 1 and stays as a lower-third whenever the unit is in frame.
- Name the phone, the app and its version, the air conditioner (brand and size from the nameplate you can read without touching), the place, and the day. Report readings exactly as measured.
- **Grade: every reading in this test is a phone-app reading, so every number is PLAUSIBLE, never VERIFIED.** Say it out loud and put it on screen. One unit in one yard on one day: never present it as how loud air conditioners are, and never compare it to a data center's level.

## Meter list

| Item | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| A phone with a sound-meter app | On an iPhone, use the free NIOSH Sound Level Meter app from the CDC, set to A-weighting, slow response. NIOSH says the app is accurate within 2 dBA in its lab on the devices it tested, and it is iOS only (NIOSH, updated Oct. 28, 2026). On Android, use any free sound-meter app, name it, and say that its accuracy isn't established. No case covering the microphone. |
| A tape measure or a pre-measured cord, 8 meters (about 26 feet) | Mark 1, 2, 4, and 8 meters with tape flags. Lay it on the ground running straight out from the unit into the most open part of the yard, away from walls and fences. |
| Four ground markers | Paper plates with "1 m," "2 m," "4 m," "8 m." They read well on camera. |
| A second phone or camera on a tripod | To film the first phone's screen and the walk. |
| Notepad | Log each reading. |

No watt meter, thermometer, or public data is needed.

## Readings to log (fill in on the day)

What to expect, so you can tell a good run from a bad one: in open ground, about 6 decibels lost for each doubling of distance (Simon Fraser University acoustics handbook; point source, free field). Close to a wall the drop will be smaller, because the wall reflects sound. Once a reading is within about 3 decibels of the background, you're mostly measuring the background; say so and stop there.

## Narration script

Read by Khaalis in his own voice, or rendered in Kokoro am_michael at speed 1.0 (then the video also carries "Narration: AI voice"). Brackets are filled in from the log; read numbers as words.

> This is a Grid and Grain Field Test. Phone app only. Nothing gets opened or touched.
>
> It's [day of week], [month day], in my yard in [town]. It's [temperature] degrees and the air is still. That's my air conditioner, a [brand and size]. It's running because the house needs it.
>
> I'm using a [phone model] and a free sound-meter app, [app name]. A phone isn't a certified sound meter, so every number you're about to see is plausible, not verified.
>
> One meter from the unit, about one long step: [reading at one meter] decibels.
>
> Two meters: [reading at two]. That's [drop] less.
>
> Four meters: [reading at four].
>
> Eight meters: [reading at eight].
>
> Now I've turned it off at the thermostat. Same four spots. The yard by itself reads about [background] decibels. So by [distance], the air conditioner was down near the background.
>
> Each time I doubled the distance, I lost about [average drop] decibels. The rule for open ground is six decibels for every doubling of distance. That's from Simon Fraser University's acoustics handbook, and it's verified. [If the drop was smaller:] Mine came in under that, and the reason's right there: that wall is bouncing sound back at me.
>
> That's one unit, one yard, one day. Yours will read differently. Try it.
>
> Here's why it matters. Look at how the distance works. Going from one meter to two took one step. Going from a quarter mile to a half mile takes a long walk, for the same six decibels. So the first few hundred feet between a machine and a house buy the most quiet. That's why an ordinance needs two things in writing: how far the equipment sits from the neighbors, and a decibel limit measured at the property line, checked by somebody independent.
>
> The worksheet is free at getruralamericadone.com. Phone app only. Know the wires. Set the terms.
