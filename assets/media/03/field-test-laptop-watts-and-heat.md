# Field Test 03: A laptop's watts and vent temperature, idle and full load (plus a phone that slows as it heats)

Module 3, *Why AI Eats Power*. Khaalis films; about 5 minutes finished. Horizontal 1920×1080 for the module page and YouTube; a vertical 1080×1920 cut of shots 5–9 for Shorts and Facebook. Can be filmed in any month, indoors.

## Safety and rules (read before filming)

- **Plug-in and handheld meters only.** The watt meter plugs into the outlet; the laptop's own charger plugs into the meter. The thermometer never touches anything. Nothing is opened: no laptop case, no charger, no battery, no phone. Not on camera, not off.
- Use the laptop's original charger. No extension cord or power strip between the outlet, the meter, and the charger.
- Leave the vents clear. Don't block a vent to make the number bigger, and don't run the laptop on a bed or a pillow.
- Aim the infrared thermometer at the vent grille only, never at anyone's eyes if it has a laser pointer.
- If the laptop or phone shows a temperature warning, stop the test and let it cool. That warning is a result; report it.
- The on-screen line **"Plug-in and handheld meters only."** appears in shot 1 and stays as a lower-third whenever a meter is in frame.
- Name the watt meter, the thermometer, the laptop, the phone, the benchmark, and the day. Report readings exactly as measured. One laptop and one phone on one day: never present them as figures for computers in general.
- **Grades:** the plug-in watt meter and the thermometer readings are reported as measured. **Every phone-app number (the benchmark score) is PLAUSIBLE, never VERIFIED,** and the video says so on screen.

## Narration script

Read by Khaalis in his own voice, or rendered in Kokoro am_michael at speed 1.0 (then the video also carries "Narration: AI voice"). Brackets are filled in from the log; read numbers as words.

> This is a Grid and Grain Field Test. Plug-in and handheld meters only.
>
> It's [day of week], [month day], at my kitchen table in [town]. The room is [room temperature] degrees. On the table: a [watt meter make and model], a [thermometer make and model], and my laptop, a [laptop make, model, and year]. Nothing gets opened today.
>
> The battery's at one hundred percent, so the meter shows what the laptop is using, not what the battery is soaking up.
>
> Sitting idle for five minutes, it draws [idle watts] watts. The air coming out of the vent reads [idle vent temperature] degrees.
>
> Now I'll make it work. This is [benchmark name]. It keeps every part of the processor busy.
>
> Ten minutes in. [Load watts] watts. The vent reads [load vent temperature] degrees. Hear the fan? That's [watts difference] more watts going in, and it's all coming back out as warm air.
>
> Here's the phone version. This is a [phone make and model] and a free benchmark app called [app name]. A phone-app number is plausible, not verified, so take it as a rough reading. First run: [score one]. Second run, right away: [score two]. Third: [score three]. The back of the phone went from [phone temperature before] degrees to [phone temperature after].
>
> [If the score fell:] Same phone, same test, [percent] percent slower. Nothing broke. The phone slowed its own clock so it wouldn't overheat. That's called throttling. Intel says its processors do the same thing to protect themselves, and that's verified.
>
> [If the score held:] The score held this time. That's the reading, so that's what we report.
>
> That's one laptop and one phone on one day. Yours will read differently. Try it.
>
> Here's why it matters. Nearly every watt that goes into a computer comes out as heat. A chip can only run as fast as that heat can be carried away. Now stack up thousands of chips, each working much harder than this laptop, in one building. Its first job is moving heat outdoors. So when somebody hands you a power number for a data center, ask whether it counts the cooling too.
>
> The questions to ask are on one page, free, at getruralamericadone.com. Plug-in and handheld meters only. Know the wires. Set the terms.
