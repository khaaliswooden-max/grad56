# Field Test 01: Outlet voltage dip when the microwave starts

Module 1, *The Wire to Your Porch*. Khaalis films; about 4 to 5 minutes finished. Horizontal 1920×1080 for the module page and YouTube; a vertical 1080×1920 cut of shots 4–7 for Shorts and Facebook.

## Safety and rules (read before filming)

- **Plug-in meters only.** The meter plugs into the outlet; the microwave plugs into the meter. Nothing is opened: no outlet cover, no panel, no appliance. Not on camera, not off.
- The meter must be rated for 15 A and the microwave must draw less than that. Check the microwave's label (input watts) before plugging it into the meter. If the input rating is above 1,700 W, don't use that microwave.
- Put a cup of water in the microwave. Never run it empty.
- The on-screen line **"Plug-in meters only."** appears in shot 1 and stays as a lower-third through every shot where the meter is in frame.
- Name the meter, the microwave, and the day on screen and in the narration. Report the readings exactly as measured. One outlet, one circuit, one day: never present it as a national figure.

>

>
> So we lost [drop] volts. Volts lost times amps flowing gives the power turned into heat on the way here: [drop] times [amps] is about [watts] watts. That heat is in the wires between this outlet and the transformer on the pole outside, most of it in the wiring inside these walls.
>
> I ran it three times, a minute apart. The readings are on screen. They're what this meter read, on this outlet, on this day. Your kitchen will be different. That's the point: try it.
>
> Here's why it matters. Heat in a wire grows with the current squared. That's why the grid moves power at hundreds of thousands of volts: the same power at a higher voltage means less current, and far less heat. Even so, about five percent of U.S. electricity is lost in delivery. That's the Energy Information Administration's average for twenty eighteen through twenty twenty-two, and it's verified.
