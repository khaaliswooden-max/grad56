# Field Test 08: Find the dead battery (a multimeter and the half-split method)

Module 8, *The Jobs Question*. Khaalis films; about 4 minutes finished. Horizontal 1920×1080 for the module page and YouTube; a vertical 1080×1920 cut of shots 5–9 for Shorts and Facebook. Can be filmed indoors in any season.

## Safety and rules (read before filming)

- **Handheld meters only, on small batteries only.** This test uses AA cells in a plastic holder: 12 volts at most, with very little current behind it. The multimeter never touches a wall outlet, a panel, an appliance, a vehicle battery, or anything connected to the house wiring. Say so on camera.
- The on-screen line **"Small batteries only. Never an outlet or a panel."** appears in shot 1 and stays as a lower-third whenever the meter is in frame.
- Set the meter to **DC volts** (the 20 V range if it isn't auto-ranging) before touching anything. Red lead in the jack marked V. Never use the amps jack in this test.
- Don't let the holder's two end wires touch each other. Don't use leaking, swollen, or rechargeable lithium cells. Recycle the dead cell afterward.
- Nothing is opened. The holder has exposed contacts between cells by design; if yours doesn't, see "Choosing the holder" below.
- Name the meter, the holder, and the day on screen and in the narration. Report readings exactly as measured.

### Choosing the holder

Use a flat, single-row 8-AA holder where the metal contacts between neighboring cells can be reached with a probe tip from the top or the side. That lets you measure across any group of cells without removing them. If the only holder you can find hides its contacts, do the test with the cells laid in a row in a length of plastic channel or between two rulers, touching end to end, and probe the exposed ends. Don't cut or modify a holder.

> This is a Grid and Grain Field Test. Small batteries only. This meter never goes near an outlet or a panel.
>
> It's [day of week], [month day], at my kitchen table in [town]. On the table: a [meter make and model], a basic multimeter set to D C volts, a holder for eight double-A batteries, seven fresh ones, and one that's used up.
>
> A fresh cell by itself reads [fresh volts] volts.
>
> Now the eight are loaded, and I don't know where the dead one is. End to end the holder reads [total volts] volts. That's low. Something in the chain is bad.
>
> I could check all eight, one at a time. A technician wouldn't. A technician splits the chain in half.
>
> Reading one. The first four read [first half] volts. The second four read [second half] volts. The [low half] half is low, so the bad cell is in there, and I never have to look at the other four.
>
> Reading two. Split that half again. These two read [pair volts]. It's in this pair.
>
> Reading three. This one cell reads [cell volts]. That's it. Cell number [n].
>
> There's my mark on the bottom. Three readings, not eight.
>
> It's called the half-split method. Every reading throws away half of what's left. Eight parts take three readings. A thousand parts in a chain take ten.
>
> I ran it three times with the dead cell moved each time. The readings are on screen, as measured.

It's learned in an apprenticeship, not picked up in a week.
>
