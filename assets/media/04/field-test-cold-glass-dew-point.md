# Field Test 04: When a cold glass sweats (dew point on the porch)

Module 4, *The Water Question*. Khaalis films; about 4 minutes finished. Horizontal 1920×1080 for the module page and YouTube; a vertical 1080×1920 cut of shots 4–8 for Shorts and Facebook. Best filmed on a mild, somewhat humid day; it can be done in any season when the outdoor dew point is above about 40°F. If the air is very dry and the ice-water glass doesn't sweat, that is a result: report it.

## Safety and rules (read before filming)

- **Handheld meters only.** A hygrometer sitting in the shade and an infrared thermometer pointed at a drinking glass. Nothing is opened, and no cooling equipment is touched: not the house air conditioner, not anyone else's. Not on camera, not off.
- Don't aim the infrared thermometer's laser at anyone's eyes.
- The on-screen line **"Handheld meters only."** appears in shot 1 and stays as a lower-third whenever a meter is in frame.
- Name the hygrometer, the thermometer, the place, and the day. Report the readings exactly as measured. One porch on one day: never present it as the climate of a region.
- **Grades:** the hygrometer and thermometer readings are reported as measured. The dew point is computed from them with the National Weather Service's public calculator; say so. If a phone weather app is shown for comparison, its number is **PLAUSIBLE**, never VERIFIED.

## Narration script

Read by Khaalis in his own voice, or rendered in Kokoro am_michael at speed 1.0 (then the video also carries "Narration: AI voice"). Brackets are filled in from the log; read numbers as words.

> This is a Grid and Grain Field Test. Handheld meters only.
>
> It's [day of week], [month day], on my porch in [town]. In the shade I've got a [hygrometer make and model]. It's been sitting fifteen minutes. The air is [air temperature] degrees, and the relative humidity is [humidity] percent.
>
> Those two numbers give you a third one, called the dew point. That's the temperature where this air can't hold its water anymore. I put both numbers into the National Weather Service's free calculator, and it says [dew point] degrees.
>
> Two glasses. One has ice water. One has water from the tap. I wrapped a band of tape around each, because a thermometer like this reads tape better than bare glass.
>
> The ice-water glass reads [ice glass temperature] degrees. That's below the dew point. The tap-water glass reads [tap glass temperature]. That's above it.
>
> Watch. [Minutes] later, the cold glass is sweating. The other one is dry. Nothing leaked through the glass. That water came out of the air.
>
> [If the cold glass stayed dry:] The cold glass stayed dry today. The air's so dry that its dew point is below even ice water. That's the reading, so that's what we report.
>
> The gap between the air and the dew point is [gap] degrees. A big gap means dry air with plenty of room for more water. A small gap means muggy air with almost none. That's one porch on one day.
>
> Here's why it matters. One way to cool a data center is to evaporate water, the same way sweat cools you. It works best when the air is dry, and it uses the most water on the hottest days of the year. The other way keeps the water sealed in pipes and blows the heat off with fans. That uses hardly any water, and more electricity. Large data centers can use up to five million gallons a day. That's from the Environmental and Energy Study Institute, June twenty twenty-five, and it's verified. Others are designed to use about what a few dozen homes do.
>
> So before you argue about gallons, ask which kind of cooling. The checklist is free at getruralamericadone.com. Handheld meters only. Know the wires. Set the terms.
