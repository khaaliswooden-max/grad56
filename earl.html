How Earl works

Six rules he doesn’t break.

Earl is an AI character we built for Grid & Grain. Picture a retired electric co-op lineman who has worked ice storms, rebuilt substations after tornadoes, and taught a lot of apprentices.

He answers only from our sourced Straight Talk library and the Field Guide modules that have opened, so he never gets ahead of what we’ve published. When the answer isn’t there, he says so.

What happens to what you type

  • We keep no copy. No chat logs, no database, no tracking of your words. The conversation lives in this browser tab and is gone when you reload or close it.
  • An AI service writes the answer. Your question and the earlier turns of the chat are sent to Anthropic, the company that makes the Claude model behind Earl. Anthropic says it deletes these within 30 days, or keeps them up to two years if its safety systems flag a conversation (its retention policy, read Nov. 26, 2026).
  • Our web host keeps ordinary request records (time and internet address), as it does for every page. They don’t include what you typed.
  • To stop misuse, we count questions per visitor using a scrambled form of the internet address, held in memory for up to a day and never written down. Ask too fast and Earl will ask you to wait.
  • Leave out anything private: names, account numbers, health details.

From Earl’s mailbag

A few he’s already answered.

Why are some lines on wood poles and some on giant steel towers?

Different jobs. The steel towers carry transmission, the interstate of the grid, roughly 69,000 volts up to 765,000. The wood poles along your road carry distribution into town, usually about 4,000 to 35,000 volts. The can on top steps it down one last time for your house.

PLAUSIBLE · typical classes; vary by utility

Why do my lights blink two or three times in a storm, then come back?

That’s a recloser. It’s a switch on the line that opens when something touches a wire, waits a moment, and tries again. Lots of faults clear themselves. If it stays out, the fault didn’t, and a crew rolls. See a line down? Stay well back and call your co-op.

PLAUSIBLE · standard utility practice

Why did it take months to get power hooked up to my new shop?

Partly the transformer. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory found utilities waiting up to two years for distribution transformers, about four times the old normal, with prices up four to nine times in three years. Steel, copper, and crews are all short.

VERIFIED · NREL, 2024
