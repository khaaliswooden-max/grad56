Ask in writing

  1. Is that megawatts or megawatt-hours, and does it cover the whole site at full build-out?
  2. What peak do you actually expect, on what schedule, and how much is under signed contract?
  3. What are your low, base, and high cases, and what gets built under each?

Why these questions

  • Power or energy. Megawatts size the substation; megawatt-hours drive the bill and the fuel. A site pulling 100 megawatts around the clock uses 876,000 megawatt-hours a year. A claim that gives only one of the two is incomplete (Module 3, Rung 5).
  • What’s counted. Nearly all the electricity a computer uses becomes heat, and removing that heat takes more electricity. A number for the computers alone is smaller than the number for the site. PLAUSIBLE Module 4 covers cooling.
  • Nameplate or real. Silicon Valley Power reports its data centers’ peak loads average about 67% of nameplate, and sites take several years to ramp up (Energy and Environmental Economics, Dec. 2025). VERIFIED
  • Signed contract. After Ohio required data centers to pay for 85% of the capacity they asked for, requests to AEP Ohio fell from 30 gigawatts to 13 (Energy and Environmental Economics, Dec. 2025). VERIFIED Requests aren’t projects.
  • Share of peak. Data centers used about 4.4% of U.S. electricity in 2023 (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory), but one project can be a large share of one utility’s system. VERIFIED
  • Low, base, and high. Berkeley Lab’s own forecast for 2028 runs from 6.7% to 12% of U.S. electricity (Dec. 2024). VERIFIED If the national experts give a range, a single local number deserves one too.
