The Three Clocks

Eleven modules, one picture. Three clocks run at three speeds, and only one of them is yours to set.

The decision it serves: writing a 12-month local plan. About 15 minutes to read, plus 10 for the Go deeper rungs.

Listen to this module Narration: AI voice

The whole module, rungs 1 to 5, read aloud. About 18 minutes. Read the script · Download MP3

Carry this

Compute grows at chip speed, power at grid speed; the gap is where you bargain.

Rung 1. Three clocks, three speeds

Everything in this Field Guide fits on three clocks.

The first is the chip clock. Demand for computing moves fast. U.S. data centers used about 4.4% of the country’s electricity in 2023. Their load had roughly tripled over the decade before, and a national laboratory projects it will double or triple again by 2028, to somewhere between 6.7% and 12% (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Dec. 2024). VERIFIED Five years, from one size to two or three times that size. That is chip speed.

The second is the grid clock. Power plants, substations, and lines move slow. A new power project now waits more than five years between asking to connect and switching on, in the regions that report data. At the end of 2025, about 2,061 gigawatts of projects were standing in that line (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, July 2026). VERIFIED Big new customers face studies and upgrades of their own (Module 1, Rung 5).

The third clock is yours. It’s the county’s calendar: the planning commission’s meeting dates, the budget hearing, the co-op’s annual meeting, the next election. Nothing gets built in your county until something on that calendar says yes.

A company on the chip clock is in a hurry. The grid it needs is not. And the permission it needs sits on a calendar that you and your neighbors control.

AnalogyThe seed company has a new variety every spring. An orchard takes years to bear. And what goes in the ground on your place this year is your decision, made on your schedule.
Where it breaksA farmer who waits a season loses one season. A county that waits can lose its turn: the project goes elsewhere, or the decision moves to the state. The third clock is yours only if you’re ready when it strikes.

Rung 2. The gap is where you bargain

Put the first two clocks side by side. One side wants power in two years. The other can deliver it in five or more. The distance between them is the gap.

That gap is where the money is, because whoever can close it fastest wins the business. It’s where the conflict is, because closing it fast tempts people to skip steps. And it’s where your room to bargain is, because a developer who needs speed has reasons to accept terms. PLAUSIBLE

Three habits follow from that.

  • Treat hurry as information. “We need a vote this month” tells you the other side’s clock is running. In Frederick County, Maryland, residents got ten days to comment on a proposal worth more than $110 million; the county executive rejected it (WYPR, Sept. 2026; Module 10). VERIFIED A good deal survives thirty more days of reading.
  • Plan in ranges. The 2028 forecast above spans nearly a factor of two. Nobody knows which end is right. A plan that only works at one end is no plan at all.
  • Write your terms before the phone rings. A county that already has its ordinance, its questions, and its priorities on paper negotiates on its own clock. A county that starts writing after the application arrives negotiates on the developer’s.

Rung 3. Three chains, three asks

Underneath the year’s twelve topics are three chains of cause and effect. Each one turns into something a community can ask for in writing.

The heat chain

Every watt a chip uses becomes heat, and moving that heat outdoors takes more power and sometimes water (Modules 3 and 4). The average data center draws about 1.52 watts for every watt that reaches its computers; weighted by size, the figure is 1.36 (Uptime Institute, Aug. 2026). VERIFIED So a watt saved at the chip saves the cooling behind it and the generation behind that.

The ask: efficiency and cooling, in the ordinance. Name the measure and where it’s taken, require it reported every year, and require the cooling design to be stated, because the design decides the water.

The capital chain

A very large customer needs a substation and lines that are paid off over decades. If that customer shrinks or leaves, the unpaid cost can land on everyone else (Module 2). As of May 2026, 23 states had approved at least one large-load tariff to prevent that (Columbia Sabin Center, in the Straight Talk library). VERIFIED

The ask: a large-load tariff with a minimum bill, collateral posted up front, an exit fee, and a contract as long as the debt.

The flexibility chain

The grid is built for its worst hour and has room in most of the others (Module 1). A customer that can cut back in the worst hours is easier and cheaper to connect (Module 7).

The ask: flexibility as a condition of connecting. Curtailment hours in the contract, batteries before more diesel engines, and limits on generator hours in the permit.

Three chains, three documents: the ordinance, the tariff, and the agreement. Module 10 explained why you want all three, each standing by itself.

Field Test: the year’s logThis month’s test is to look back at the other eleven. If you ran them, lay your readings side by side: the volts your outlet lost when the microwave started, the dishwasher cycle at two prices, the laptop’s watts idle and busy, the dew point on the porch, the condenser’s sound at four distances, the words a laptop wrote per joule, the window unit’s watts through the peak hour, the dead battery you found by halving, the delay at the far fence, the mesh point at the modem, and the dishwasher against the sun. For each one, write three things: what you measured, with what, and whether it matched what the module said. Where it didn’t match, that’s the interesting line. Phone-app readings are estimates. PLAUSIBLE One house is never a national figure. Plug-in and handheld meters only. Never open equipment.

Rung 4. A twelve-month plan, quarter by quarter

A county plan for this isn’t a study. It’s a short list of facts to gather, rules to write, and dates to hit, with a name next to each. Here is one way to lay it out. The order matters more than the months. PLAUSIBLE

First quarter: know where you stand

  • Fill in the Know Your Utility sheet (Module 1): who owns the wires, when the board is elected, and your system’s peak demand.
  • Ask the utility, in writing, how many large-load requests are pending and how big the largest is as a share of that peak.
  • Ask whether anyone in county government has signed a nondisclosure agreement (Module 10).
  • Mark the year’s fixed dates: budget adoption, planning commission meetings, the co-op annual meeting, filing deadlines for board seats.

Second quarter: write the rules

  • Water: a disclosure requirement covering cooling type, peak and average gallons, source, and a drought clause (Module 4).
  • Noise and land: a decibel limit at the property line, setbacks, generator test hours, and third-party monitoring (Module 5).
  • Efficiency: the yearly reporting from the heat chain above.

Spring is planning and zoning season in most places. An ordinance written before any application is on file is ordinary lawmaking. One written after can look like it was aimed at a single company, and may be challenged that way. PLAUSIBLE

Third quarter: set the terms

  • The tariff: if your utility has no large-load tariff, ask the co-op board or the state commission for one, using the Module 2 checklist.
  • Flexibility: the curtailment and battery terms from Module 7.
  • Jobs: local-hire targets, apprenticeships, and a community college partner (Module 8).

Fourth quarter: decide what you want

  • Fill in the benefit agreement term sheet with your own priorities before anyone offers theirs (Module 10).
  • Name the three things your community is shortest on. For many rural places that’s the hospital, broadband, and a way for young people to train and stay (Modules 6, 8, and 9).
  • Ask the neighbors whether any public computing is worth pooling (Module 11).
  • Set next year’s dates, and publish the plan.

Triggers

Most counties will never get the call. So the plan should say what moves the county from watching to acting. Three reasonable triggers: a pending request above a set share of the utility’s peak; a rezoning or annexation inquiry for a large tract; a request that any official sign a nondisclosure agreement. SPECULATIVE When one trips, the plan’s owner calls a public meeting. That’s all a trigger has to do.

AnalogyA county emergency plan doesn’t predict the tornado. It says who opens the shelter, who has the keys, and what siren means go.
Where it breaksA tornado isn’t negotiating with you. A developer is, and will read your plan. That’s fine. A published plan tells serious developers what a yes looks like and saves both sides time.

Rung 5. Planning when nobody knows

The numbers in Rung 1 came with a warning: the 2028 range runs from 6.7% to 12%. People who study this for a living disagree by nearly a factor of two about a date that is close. Past that date, they disagree by more.

Why the forecasts are wide

History gives reasons to doubt both extremes. From 2010 to 2018, the world’s data centers did about 6.5 times as much computing while their electricity use rose only about 6%, because efficiency improved almost as fast as demand (Masanet et al., 2020, in the Straight Talk library). VERIFIED AI broke that balance after 2017. Whether efficiency catches up again, nobody can say. SPECULATIVE

The grid clock has the same uncertainty. The line of projects waiting to connect shrank by 10% in 2025, with more than 750 gigawatts withdrawn. Of everything that asked to connect from 2000 to 2020, about 13% had been built by the end of 2025 and 75% had dropped out (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, July 2026). VERIFIED Most of what is announced never arrives.

What a plan can do about that

Ask for three cases, not one. Any forecast your utility or a developer shows you should come as a low, a base, and a high. Then test each term in your plan against all three.

  • If the boom keeps going, the risk is strain: on rates, water, land, and the grid. The heat and flexibility asks protect you.
  • If the boom ends, the risk is being left with the bill: a substation built for a customer who never came, or left early. The capital ask protects you. A minimum bill, collateral, and an exit fee matter most in exactly the future where the optimists were wrong. PLAUSIBLE
  • Either way, an enforcement clause is worth the same. Module 10’s test doesn’t depend on the forecast.

That’s the reason to write terms that hold in every case, and not to bet the county on one.

Where the critics were right this year

This guide has tried to say so each time. Permanent jobs are few (Module 8). Tax breaks can erase much of the promised revenue (Module 10). Noise complaints are real, and so is local water strain from some cooling designs (Modules 4 and 5). Deals are often made in secret (Module 10). Rate risk is real where power is tight (Module 2). None of that is settled by enthusiasm. All of it can be addressed in writing, and that has been the argument of the whole year.

Still open SPECULATIVEWill the three clocks stay this far apart? Faster grid studies, on-site power, or a slowdown in AI spending could each narrow the gap, and with it the leverage. Will states leave these decisions with counties, or take them? Does a published county plan attract better proposals, or just fewer? We don’t know. If your county writes one, tell us what happened, and we’ll publish it with the grade it earns.

Straight Talk

“The AI boom will be over soon.”

It depends, and your plan shouldn’t.

It might be. Forecasts for 2028 differ by nearly a factor of two, from 6.7% to 12% of U.S. electricity (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Dec. 2024), and most projects that ask to connect to the grid are never built: 75% of the capacity requested from 2000 to 2020 was withdrawn (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, July 2026). VERIFIED

But “it’ll blow over” isn’t a plan either. If the boom ends, the communities hurt most will be the ones that built for a customer and didn’t secure the bill. PLAUSIBLE

Ask for it in writing: low, base, and high cases in any forecast, and terms that protect you in all three.

What the year was for

Infrastructure is a means. The end is a hospital that stays open, a farm that stays profitable, and a kid who can build a career without leaving home.

None of that comes from a building at the edge of town. It comes from what a community asks for and gets in writing: broadband that stays, a training program with a real employer at the end of it, technology money for the clinic and the schools, a co-op whose members aren’t paying for someone else’s substation. The opportunity is not the building. It is the leverage.
