The Jobs Question

Every data center proposal arrives with a jobs number. Here’s how to take it apart, what the research says, and how to make sure some of those jobs go to people who live here.

The decision it serves: judging job promises and building a training pipeline. About 12 minutes to read, plus 10 for the Go deeper rungs.

Carry this

Construction jobs are many and temporary; operating jobs are few and good. Plan for both, and put the hiring in writing.

Rung 1. Two kinds of jobs

When a data center is proposed, the first number in the room is usually a jobs number. Before you weigh it, split it in two.

Construction jobs build the place: site crews, ironworkers, concrete, electricians, pipefitters. There are a lot of them, and they end when the building is finished. Many of the workers travel from job to job and go home when it’s done.

Operating jobs run the place afterward: technicians who keep the power and cooling going, people who swap failed parts, security, and managers. There are far fewer of these, and they tend to pay well and last.

Both kinds are real. They are not the same kind of promise. A job that lasts eighteen months and a job that lasts twenty years shouldn’t be added together into one number, and when they are, it’s fair to ask for them back apart.

AnalogyBuilding a grain elevator takes a big crew for a season. Running it takes a manager and a few hands. Nobody in town confuses the two, and nobody thinks the elevator is worthless because it only employs five.
Where it breaksThe elevator buys what the county grows, so its money circulates here. A data hall’s customers and most of its suppliers are somewhere else. Its value to the county has to be arranged on purpose: taxes, training, fiber, and the terms in this module.

Rung 2. What the studies find

Two research teams looked at what happened in counties that got data centers, and they agree on more than they disagree.

Georgia Tech researchers matched individual facilities to county economic records. They found that rural facilities typically employ few permanent workers, often fewer than 100, and that many specialized services are brought in from outside the county. Counties in metro areas saw gains in jobs and new businesses. Counties outside metro areas saw few measurable gains beyond a small drop in unemployment. Across all host counties, employment rose about 0.9% and wages about 1.1% in the first three years (Yue and Zeng, Georgia Tech, as reported by Futurity, July 2026). VERIFIED

Brookings researchers compared about 1,500 built facilities with 52 that were announced and then canceled. That comparison matters, because counties that attract data centers were already growing faster than their neighbors. They found large percentage gains in two narrow industries over the first ten years, data processing up 56% and telecommunications up 43%, which works out to roughly 100 to 200 jobs in a typical county. Wages didn’t change. Home prices rose 2% to 5% (Bahar and Wright, Brookings Institution, updated Aug. 2026). VERIFIED Brookings discloses that it receives unrestricted donations from Amazon, Google, Meta, and Microsoft, and says the findings are the authors’ own.

So the honest summary is this. The jobs are real, they number in the dozens to low hundreds, and in a rural county they don’t automatically go to people who live there.

Field Test: find the dead battery the way a technician wouldMuch of an operating job is finding the one bad part in a long chain. Put eight AA batteries in an eight-cell holder, with one used-up battery hidden among them. Checking them one at a time takes up to eight readings. A technician uses the half-split method instead: measure across the first four with a $20 multimeter. The half that reads low holds the bad one. Split that half, then split again. Three readings, every time. Small batteries only. A multimeter never goes near a wall outlet, a panel, or a car battery in this test.

Rung 3. Who gets the good jobs

The operating jobs go to people with a trade: electricians, heating and cooling mechanics, controls technicians. Whether those people come from your county depends on whether your county trains them.

Take electricians. The typical way in is a high school diploma and a four- or five-year apprenticeship, which is a paid job with classroom instruction alongside it. Median pay was $63,190 a year in May 2025. The Bureau of Labor Statistics expects the trade to grow 9% from 2025 to 2035, with about 72,700 openings a year (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Aug. 2026). VERIFIED That is a national figure for all electricians, not a data center wage.

Veterans have a head start and a benefit. Someone who qualifies for the GI Bill can use it during an apprenticeship or on-the-job training, receiving a monthly payment for living expenses, and under the Post-9/11 GI Bill, money for books and supplies (U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs). VERIFIED

And the industry needs the help. In Uptime Institute’s 2026 survey of data center operators, more than half said they had difficulty finding qualified candidates for open positions (Uptime Institute, July 2026). VERIFIED

That shortage is the county’s leverage. An operator that can’t find technicians has its own reason to pay for a training program at the community college. The time to ask is before the permit, and the place to put the answer is the agreement.

Go deeper

The first three rungs explain which jobs are real and who gets them. The next two explain how a jobs number is built, and how to write a hiring commitment that holds up. They get more technical, but every term is defined. Skip them and you can still pass the self-check; read them and you can question an economic impact study at a public hearing.

Rung 4. How a jobs number is built

The figure in a press release usually comes from an economic impact study paid for by the developer or an economic development agency. These studies follow standard methods, and the methods leave room. Four questions take most of the air out of a large number. PLAUSIBLE as a description of standard practice.

Is it jobs, or job-years?

A job-year is one person working for one year. A project that employs 500 people for two years of construction can be described as 1,000 job-years, and somewhere between the study and the headline the word “years” tends to fall off.

Direct, indirect, or induced?

Direct jobs are on the project’s own payroll. Indirect jobs are at its suppliers. Induced jobs come from workers spending their pay at the diner and the hardware store. The second and third are estimated with a multiplier, and a multiplier built for a metro area overstates what happens in a county where the suppliers and the big-box stores are somewhere else. The Georgia Tech finding that specialized services come from outside the county is that same leak, measured. PLAUSIBLE

Compared with what?

A county that lands a data center was often growing already. Comparing it with a county that never had a chance credits the data center with growth that was coming anyway. That is why the Brookings team compared built projects with canceled ones, and why they warn that a simple before-and-after comparison overstates the job effect (Bahar and Wright, 2026). VERIFIED

What did each job cost?

Divide the tax breaks by the permanent jobs. The Brookings study found state incentives worth about 2% of construction investment in counties with the largest, company-owned facilities, and about 62% in counties whose facilities lease space to others (Bahar and Wright, 2026). VERIFIED The same word, “incentive,” covers a rounding error and most of the building.

None of this means the study is dishonest. It means the headline is the top of a range, and the county is entitled to see the range.

AnalogyA seed dealer’s yield number comes from a test plot: good ground, perfect rain, every input on time. It’s a real number. It isn’t your field.
Where it breaksYou can plant a strip and check the dealer’s number in one season. A county can’t run the project twice. The only check it gets is the one it writes into the agreement: report the hires every year, and pay something back if they fall short.

Rung 5. Writing a workforce clause that holds

What a clause needs

A hiring promise in a slide deck binds no one. A term in a signed agreement can. Reasoned from how these agreements are built, a workforce clause holds when it does five things. PLAUSIBLE

  • Defines “local.” Residents of the county, or of named neighboring counties, on the day they’re hired. Without a definition, a worker who rents a room for the job counts.
  • Sets separate targets for construction and for operations, as a share of hours worked or of positions filled.
  • Funds the pipeline early. Money and equipment for the community college or the high school career center, starting before construction, so graduates exist when hiring begins.
  • Requires apprentices. A share of construction hours worked by registered apprentices, so the project leaves trained people behind.
  • Reports and enforces. A yearly public report of hires by home county, and a consequence, such as a reduced abatement, if a target is missed.

What one county’s offer looked like

In September 2026 a developer offered Frederick County, Maryland, $110 million in community benefits for a 2,100-acre data center campus. The package included $14.5 million for a workforce training center and career and technical education. It did not include a local hiring requirement. In return the developer asked the county not to change zoning in ways that could hurt the project for eight years. After hundreds of public comments, the county executive rejected the agreement two weeks later and extended a moratorium on data center applications to July 1, 2027 (WYPR, Sept. 15, 2026; Maryland Association of Counties, Sept. 2, 2026). VERIFIED

Two lessons sit in that story, whichever side you’d have taken. Money for a training center is not a commitment to hire the people it trains. And every benefit has a price on the other side of the page; read what the county is asked to give up.

Can a county require local hiring?

It depends on the state, and on whether the term is a condition of a permit or part of a negotiated agreement tied to an incentive. A county can usually attach conditions to money it’s giving up; what it can impose outright is narrower. PLAUSIBLE This is a question for the county attorney before the first meeting, not after the last one.

Tie it to the abatement

Most of a county’s leverage is the tax break. If the project is getting one, the hiring targets belong in the same document, on the same schedule, with the break shrinking if the targets are missed. The Straight Talk library has both ends of what abatements can do: in Loudoun County, Virginia, tax on data center equipment alone was about 29% of the county’s local tax revenue in fiscal 2024, while El Paso granted an 80% abatement over 35 years. VERIFIED

Still open SPECULATIVEDoes a training program paid for by one operator keep its graduates in the county, or train them for jobs two states away? As halls automate more of their own maintenance, do the operating jobs shrink further? Could a rural county do better by training its people for the regional construction circuit than by counting on one building? Nobody has settled these. Questions for researchers, and for your community college.

Straight Talk

“It’ll bring hundreds of jobs.”

Wrong for permanent jobs.

Rural facilities often employ fewer than 100 permanent workers, and specialized services come from outside the county (Georgia Tech, as reported by Futurity, July 2026). VERIFIED Construction jobs are real but temporary.

They’re right about this part: the permanent jobs that do exist are skilled, and operators say they can’t find enough people to fill them.

Ask for it in writing: local-hire targets, apprenticeships, and a community college partnership, in the agreement itself.

What this means for your family

The question to carry is whether a kid from your county can get one of these jobs without leaving, and whether someone mid-career can retrain into one.

That depends less on the data center than on what is in place around it: a program at the community college, an apprenticeship with open seats, a counselor who knows the path exists. A veteran with a logistics or electrical specialty is closer to these jobs than most, and has a benefit that pays during the training.

A trade learned on one project doesn’t expire when the project does. Electricians, mechanics, and controls technicians are needed at the hospital, the co-op, the water plant, and the school. Training is the one benefit that stays if the building doesn’t.
