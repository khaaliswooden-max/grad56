Noise, Light, and Land

Noise is the complaint neighbors actually live with. Here’s how sound travels, what counties are already writing into law, and how to tell a rule you can enforce from one you can’t.

The decision it serves: writing or reviewing a zoning ordinance. About 15 minutes to read, plus 10 for the Go deeper rungs.

Carry this

A limit at the property line beats a promise in a press release.

Rung 1. How sound fades

Modules 3 and 4 followed the heat out of the building. Moving that heat takes fans, pumps, and chillers, and all of them make noise. So does the row of backup generators out back. A data center’s sound is mostly the sound of its cooling.

Loudness is measured in decibels. A whisper is about 30. A humming refrigerator is about 40. A normal talking voice at arm’s length is 65 to 80. Sounds at 85 or more can damage hearing over time (National Institutes of Health, citing the CDC). VERIFIED You’ll usually see the unit written dBA. The A means the meter is tuned to hear roughly the way a person does.

Sound fades as you walk away from it, by a fixed rule: out in the open, the level drops 6 decibels each time you double your distance from the source (Simon Fraser University acoustics handbook). VERIFIED for a single source with nothing reflecting the sound.

Notice what that rule does. The first doubling is cheap: from 10 feet to 20 feet. The eighth doubling is expensive: from a quarter mile to a half mile. Distance helps a lot close in and less and less farther out. That’s why where the machines sit on the property matters as much as how big the property is.

AnalogyA pebble dropped in a pond. The ripple is tall where the pebble lands and flattens as the ring spreads, because the same energy is stretched around a bigger and bigger circle.
Where it breaksA pond is flat and empty. Real sound bounces off buildings, bends over hills, carries farther on a still night, and adds up when there are a hundred machines instead of one pebble.

Rung 2. Measure it at the property line

You’ll hear two kinds of claims. From opponents: “They’re 96 decibels, 24 hours a day.” From developers: “You won’t hear a thing.” Both skip the only question that matters: how loud, measured where?

The city of Linn Valley, Kansas, put it plainly while studying its own ordinance. Levels right next to machines or inside equipment rooms can be very high, but those readings don’t establish the level at a property line or a home (City of Linn Valley, updated Aug. 2026). VERIFIED Next to a big generator, 100 decibels is possible. At a house a half mile off, it’s a different number. Neither number is a lie. Only one of them is about your bedroom.

So the rule worth having names a number and a place: no more than so many decibels, measured at the property line. Counties are writing these now.

  • Sanford, North Carolina: data centers may not exceed 65 dBA at the perimeter of their property, and the city may order independent testing (NC Newsline, July 2026). VERIFIED
  • Henry County, Virginia: 50 dBA at the property line, a sound study before construction, a test within 90 days of full operation, and testing every year for the life of the facility (WDBJ, Aug. 2026). VERIFIED
  • Spencer County, Indiana: an ordinance text posted by the county sets 50 dBA at the property line, and says that if the place is already louder than that, the data center may not add to it (Spencer County Ordinance 2026-07). VERIFIED as the posted text; the copy we read was unsigned.

Fifteen decibels separates 50 from 65. That’s a big gap. By the rule in Rung 1, it’s the difference between standing at one spot and standing more than five times farther away. Which number is right for your county is a local choice. What matters is that there is a number, a place it’s measured, and someone other than the operator doing the measuring.

Where the critics are right. Noise complaints are real, and a limit on paper doesn’t end them. Neighbors in Vineland, New Jersey, have sued over round-the-clock noise from a data center (in the Straight Talk library). VERIFIED A limit nobody tests is a press release with a number in it.

Field Test: walk away from an air conditionerOn a still day, stand 1 meter, about one long step, from your own home’s outdoor air conditioner unit while it’s running. Open a sound-meter app and write down the reading. Then read it again at 2 meters, 4 meters, and 8 meters, walking straight out into the yard. Turn the unit off at the thermostat and take the background level at the same four spots. In open ground you should lose close to 6 decibels each time you double the distance, until the reading sinks into the background. Next to a wall you’ll lose less. A phone reading is PLAUSIBLE, never VERIFIED: the federal government’s own app claims accuracy within 2 decibels only on the phones it was tested on. Phone app only. Don’t touch, open, or reach into the unit, and measure only on your own property.

Rung 3. The working parts of an ordinance

A noise number is one line. The ordinances passed in 2026 pair it with a handful of others, and each one does a job.

  • Setbacks. Distance between the equipment and the neighbors. Henry County requires at least 1,000 feet from land that isn’t zoned industrial (WDBJ, Aug. 2026). Spencer County’s text requires at least 500 feet from homes and recommends 1,000 (Ordinance 2026-07). VERIFIED Remember Rung 1: the first few hundred feet buy the most quiet.
  • Screening. Berms and evergreens along roads and neighbors’ lines. Spencer County’s text asks for a screen at least 20 feet tall once it’s grown. VERIFIED
  • Light. Outdoor lights aimed inward and downward and shielded, so they don’t spill onto neighbors or light up the night sky (Spencer County Ordinance 2026-07). VERIFIED
  • Generator test hours. Backup generators have to be run regularly to prove they work. Henry County limits routine testing to weekdays, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. (WDBJ, Aug. 2026). VERIFIED
  • Monitoring. A sound study by an outside engineer before construction, a test after opening, and repeat testing. The operator pays.
  • The end of the story. Henry County requires money set aside to cover taking the facility down someday, with the estimate reviewed every three years (WDBJ, Aug. 2026). VERIFIED

All of these cost little to require before a site is designed. After the concrete is poured, moving a generator yard 500 feet is nearly impossible. PLAUSIBLE The time to write it down is before the vote.

Go deeper

The first three rungs give you the limit, the place to measure, and the parts of an ordinance. The next two cover what a simple decibel number misses, and who actually has the power to enforce any of it. They’re more technical, but every term is defined. You can pass the self-check without them.

Rung 4. The hum a meter can miss

People who live near data centers often describe a low hum or a steady tone, not a roar. A standard reading can understate both.

Low-pitched sound

An A-weighted meter (dBA) is built to be less sensitive to low-pitched sound, because human ears are too. But big fans and engines put out a lot of low-pitched sound. A thorough study also uses C-weighting (dBC), which counts the low end more fully, or looks at each band of pitch separately (City of Linn Valley, updated Aug. 2026). VERIFIED

Spencer County’s text handles it this way: low-pitched or tonal sound may not exceed the dBC level that was there before construction (Ordinance 2026-07). VERIFIED That only works if someone measured the place before construction. The baseline reading is the cheapest study a county will ever commission, and it can’t be done afterward.

A single tone

A steady whine at one pitch is more annoying than a broad whoosh at the same decibel level. PLAUSIBLE Henry County’s rule says equipment may not produce a clearly audible pure tone at or beyond a residential property line (WDBJ, Aug. 2026). VERIFIED

Averages and adding up

Two more traps. First, many limits are written as an average over time, called Leq. An average can hide a generator test that’s loud for one hour a week. Ask whether the limit is an average or a maximum. Second, decibels don’t add like ordinary numbers. Two identical machines aren’t twice the decibels of one; they’re 3 decibels more. Ten identical machines are 10 more. VERIFIED by arithmetic. So a study has to model the whole site running at once on its hottest day, not one unit.

AnalogyA bathroom scale that only weighs what’s above your knees. It gives a real number, and it’s the wrong number for the question. A dBA-only study of a low hum is that scale.
Where it breaksThe scale is simply wrong. A-weighting is right for most everyday sound, which is why it’s the standard. It just needs a second reading alongside it when the source is big, slow machinery.

Rung 5. Who decides, and who enforces

Zoning is local

Rates are set by a state commission or a co-op board. Land use is usually set at the county or the city. That makes zoning the lever closest to home. Henry County’s process shows the steps: a new zoning district for data centers, a rezoning with public hearings before the planning commission and the board of supervisors, and then a special use permit with its own hearing, where conditions can be added (WDBJ, Aug. 2026). VERIFIED States differ in how much they leave to local government, so check with your county attorney.

Generators: what federal rules cover, and what they don’t

A data center keeps enough backup generators to carry its whole load through a grid outage, plus spares, so a large site can have dozens. PLAUSIBLE Federal air rules let an emergency engine run up to 100 hours a year for maintenance checks and readiness testing, and put no time limit on running in a real emergency (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Sept. 2013). VERIFIED

So “they’ll run diesel all the time” overstates it for emergency engines. But the federal rule is about air pollution, not noise, and not the time of day. One hundred hours each across dozens of engines is a lot of testing. PLAUSIBLE When those hours fall is left to you. That’s the weekday, 9-to-5 clause.

Enforcement is the hard part

An ordinance is only as strong as the county’s ability to check it. A rural county rarely owns a certified sound meter or employs an acoustical engineer. The ordinances above answer that by making the operator pay for third-party testing on a schedule and after credible complaints, and by treating each day of a continuing violation as a separate offense (Spencer County Ordinance 2026-07). VERIFIED

Still open SPECULATIVEDoes very low-pitched sound from large cooling plants affect health at the levels neighbors report, or mainly sleep and comfort? What happens to a 50-decibel limit when three campuses sit on adjoining parcels, each one compliant alone? And is a 1,000-foot setback enough for the larger machines now being designed? Nobody has settled these. Questions for researchers, and for your planning board.

Straight Talk

“They’re 96 decibels, 24 hours a day.”

Overstated as a blanket claim. Noise complaints are real.

Readings taken next to machines don’t equal levels at a home (City of Linn Valley). Neighbors in Vineland, New Jersey, have sued over round-the-clock noise. Sanford, North Carolina, caps data centers at 65 dBA at the edge of the property (NC Newsline). VERIFIED

So don’t argue about the 96. Argue about the number at your fence.

Ask for it in writing: a dBA limit at the property line, low-frequency measurement, setbacks, generator test hours, and third-party monitoring. Read the full entry.

At your fence line

Power bills and water tables are things you read about. Noise and light you live with, every night, at your own house. That’s why this is the module to bring to a planning board meeting.

Two things a resident can do before any project is approved. First, ask the county to commission a baseline sound reading at the nearest homes, in dBA and dBC, day and night. It’s the “before” that makes every later complaint provable. Second, ask where on the site the cooling equipment and generators will sit. The same building can be a good neighbor or a bad one depending on which side the machines face.

And if you farm or own land nearby: setbacks, screening, and shielded lights are cheap to require at the start and costly to add later. Ask for them now.

AI character

How loud is 65 dBA?

“About a normal talking voice at arm’s length. Doesn’t sound like much, does it? Now picture that voice outside your bedroom window all night, every night, and it never takes a breath. That’s the difference between loud and constant. A number tells you how loud. It doesn’t tell you how long, or how low the pitch is, or whether it’s your fence they measured at. So when somebody quotes you decibels, ask three things. Measured where? Averaged or peak? And who’s holding the meter? Then ask for it in writing.”
