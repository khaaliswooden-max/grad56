AI on the Farm

Precision farming tools are computers making decisions about your ground. Here’s where those computers sit, what they need from your signal, and what to read before you sign.

The decision it serves: choosing precision-ag tools, and where the computing should happen. About 12 minutes to read, plus 10 for the Go deeper rungs.

Carry this

Some decisions can’t wait for a round trip to the cloud. Before you buy, ask what the tool does with no signal, and who ends up holding your data.

Rung 1. A decision that can’t wait

Precision agriculture means treating each part of a field according to what that part needs, instead of treating the whole field the same. A planter that changes its seeding rate as the soil changes. A sprayer that sprays the weed and skips the bare ground. A yield monitor that maps where the crop came in heavy and where it didn’t.

Every one of those is a computer making a decision. The question this module asks is where that computer sits.

Some decisions are slow. Which hybrid to plant next spring can be worked out over the winter, on a big computer a thousand miles away. Call that the cloud: somebody else’s data hall, reached over the internet.

Some decisions are fast. A sprayer rolling across a field has a fraction of a second to decide whether the green thing under its boom is a weed. That decision has to be made on the machine. Engineers call a computer that sits where the work happens the edge.

AnalogyYou’d mail a soil sample to the lab and wait a week for the report. You wouldn’t mail the lab a question about whether to swerve around a rock.
Where it breaksThe lab and the tractor seat aren’t really separate. The fast decision on the machine is made by a model that was built, slowly, in a data hall, from other people’s fields, and the machine sends its own records back afterward. Edge and cloud are two ends of one system.

Rung 2. What the tools do, and who is using them

Start with a tool that works. One manufacturer’s camera-guided sprayer carries cameras and processors on the boom, identifies weeds as it passes, and opens individual nozzles only over them. The company reports its customers ran it on more than 5 million acres in 2025 and cut their use of non-residual herbicide by nearly 50% on average, at speeds up to 15 miles an hour (Deere & Co. news release, Nov. 2025). VERIFIED as the company’s own report of its own product; no independent audit is cited.

Now the wider picture, which is thinner. In a 2025 federal survey, 22% of U.S. farms used precision agriculture practices, down from 27% in 2023 (USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, Aug. 2025). VERIFIED The survey counts farms of every size.

Congress’s auditors looked at why. The benefits they list are real: the same yield with less fertilizer, herbicide, fuel, and water, and less runoff. The obstacles are real too: equipment that costs more than a smaller farm can justify, brands that won’t work together, and farmers who don’t trust what happens to their data (U.S. Government Accountability Office, Jan. 2024). VERIFIED

They’re right to be cautious. A tool that pays on 5,000 acres may never pay on 300, and a subscription is a cost every year whether or not the crop comes in.

Rung 3. Coverage on the map, signal in the field

Whatever a tool decides on the machine, it still needs a connection sooner or later: to download its maps, upload its records, and get its updates.

Here is how farms connect. In the same 2025 survey, 85% of U.S. farms had internet access. Counting every type a farm uses, 74% connected through a cellular data plan, 55% through a wired broadband line, and 24% by satellite (USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service, Aug. 2025). VERIFIED

Two cautions about numbers like that. First, they describe the farm, which usually means the house and the shop. They say nothing about the far corner of the back forty. Second, the federal broadband maps count where a provider says service is available; the map can show coverage where a tractor cab shows no bars. PLAUSIBLE The only reading that counts is one you take yourself, in the place the machine will be working.

Two things to measure. Speed is how much data moves per second. Latency is the delay for one round trip, in thousandths of a second, or milliseconds. A tool that uploads a yield map overnight cares about speed. A tool that expects an answer back while the machine is moving cares about latency.

Field Test: latency at the house, the field edge, and the far fencePut a free speed-test app on your phone. Stand at the house and run it three times; write down download, upload, and latency. Do the same at the edge of a field, and again at the fence farthest from the road. Note the carrier and the number of bars each time. Now you have a coverage map nobody can argue with, made by the person who farms the ground. Phone-app readings are PLAUSIBLE, not lab measurements, and they change with weather and time of day. Phone only. Walk or drive the field as you normally would, and never run the test from a moving tractor seat.

Rung 4. The arithmetic of a round trip

How far does the machine move while it waits?

Take the sprayer at 15 miles an hour. That is 22 feet every second.

Now suppose it had to ask a distant data hall whether to spray. One widely cited measurement put the average round trip from 260 points around the world to the nearest large cloud site at 74 milliseconds, before adding the wireless hop at either end (Satyanarayanan, Computer, Jan. 2017). VERIFIED The measurement is an older one, and a rural cellular link adds delay of its own.

In 74 milliseconds the sprayer travels 22 feet per second × 0.074 seconds, which is about 1.6 feet. VERIFIED as arithmetic on the two figures above. The weed is behind the nozzle before the answer arrives. And that is the best case, with a signal. With no signal there is no answer at all.

So the decision is made on the boom. Not because the cloud is weak, but because distance is delay, and a moving machine can’t spend it.

Send the answer, not the pictures

The same paper names a second reason to compute at the edge: a nearby computer can boil a flood of raw data down to a small result and send only that (Satyanarayanan, 2017). VERIFIED By the company’s account, that sprayer’s cameras scan more than 2,500 square feet of ground every second. No rural connection carries that much video. What the machine can send, later, from the shop, is the summary: where it sprayed and how much. PLAUSIBLE

A latency budget

Engineers sort jobs by how long they can wait. You can do the same with any tool you’re offered. PLAUSIBLE as a way of sorting, not a standard.

  • Thousandths of a second. Steering, spraying, anything tied to a moving machine. This has to run on the machine.
  • Seconds to minutes. A grain cart finding the combine, an alert that a pivot has stopped. A local link is enough; the cloud is optional.
  • Hours to seasons. Yield analysis, seed selection, marketing. The cloud is fine, and a slow connection overnight will do.

A tool that puts a first-row job on a third-row connection will fail in the field, and the field is where you’ll find out.

AnalogyA combine operator and a grain cart driver work by hand signals and a glance, because that’s fast enough. The elevator’s price comes by phone, because that can wait a minute. Nobody runs the hand signals through the elevator.
Where it breaksTwo people in a field can always see each other. A machine at the edge only knows what it was taught before it left the shop. If the weeds changed this year, it finds out when someone updates it, and that update comes down the same thin connection.

Rung 5. Who holds the harvest of data

Ownership is a principle; control is a contract

Every pass across a field now produces a record: what was planted, sprayed, and harvested, where, and when. Whose is it?

The farm groups and equipment companies that wrote the industry’s Privacy and Security Principles for Farm Data put it plainly: farmers own the information generated on their operations. But that is a principle the industry agreed to, not a rule of law. The same extension guide that explains it says what actually governs is the written agreement between the farmer and the technology company, and that ownership gets murky when a landlord, a tenant, and a custom applicator all have a hand in the same field (Ohio State University Extension, 2018). VERIFIED

That guide lists what to look for in the agreement: who may collect and see the data, why, whether you can opt out of pieces, whether you can take your data to another company, whether it can be sold or shared, how long it’s kept, what happens when you cancel, and whether the company is barred from using it to trade in commodity markets (Ohio State University Extension, 2018). VERIFIED The County Kit turns that list into a one-page sheet.

Why this matters more with AI

An AI model is built from data. The sprayer that knows a weed from a soybean learned it from pictures of many farmers’ fields. That is a fair trade if the terms say so. Congress’s auditors found that concerns over data sharing and ownership are themselves an obstacle to using AI in agriculture: farmers worry about losing a competitive edge, about security, and about drawing regulatory attention (U.S. Government Accountability Office, Jan. 2024). VERIFIED

The same report names the other lock: without common standards, one brand’s equipment and records may not work with another’s (GAO, Jan. 2024). VERIFIED Data you can’t move is data you don’t control, whatever the contract calls it. PLAUSIBLE

What a county has to do with it

Module 6 made the case that a hospital’s AI is only as good as its connection. A farm’s is the same. When fiber or a tower comes to a county as part of a larger project, where it runs decides which farms can use these tools. PLAUSIBLE A route that passes the school, the clinic, and the farm-to-market roads is worth asking for by name.

Still open SPECULATIVECould farmers pool their data in a cooperative they own, the way they pooled grain, and license it on their own terms? Would a small shared computer at the co-op or the elevator serve a neighborhood of farms better than a signal to a distant hall? Does precision equipment ever pay on a small farm if it’s shared or hired by the acre? Nobody has settled these. Questions for researchers, and for your extension office.

Straight Talk

“My farm data is mine automatically.”

It depends on what you signed.

The industry’s own principles say farmers own the information generated on their operations. Those are principles, not law; the written agreement decides who can collect, use, share, and keep the data (Ohio State University Extension, 2018). VERIFIED

They’re right about this part: farmers’ distrust of how their data is handled is well founded enough that federal auditors list it as a barrier to adoption (GAO, Jan. 2024). VERIFIED

Ask for it in writing: who can see the data, whether it can be sold, whether you can take it with you, and what gets deleted when you leave.

What this means for your family

For a farm family the question is whether a tool earns more than it costs, on your acres, with your signal. Three things to settle before the check is written: what it does with no connection, what it costs every year after the first, and what happens to your records if you switch brands or quit.

For the kid in 4-H or FFA, this is a career that doesn’t require leaving. Somebody has to install, calibrate, and repair these machines, and that somebody needs to know both a wiring diagram and a field.

And for the county, farm connectivity is infrastructure, the same as a bridge that can carry a loaded grain truck. It belongs on the list when anyone is negotiating over fiber.

Ask Earl

AI character

The map says I’ve got coverage. Why is there no signal in my back forty?

“Because the map and the field are two different things. The map shows where a company says it can serve. It doesn’t know about your creek bottom or the hill between you and the tower. So don’t argue with the map, measure the field. Take your phone to the house, the field edge, and the far fence, run a speed test three times at each, and write down what you get. That’s a plausible reading, not a lab number, but it’s yours, and it’s from the spot that matters. Then before you buy anything that needs a signal, ask the dealer one thing: what does it do out there when it’s got none? Ask for that in writing. And do your testing on foot. Never fool with a phone from a moving tractor seat.”
