Who Pays for the Substation?

A big new customer needs a substation built before it uses a single kilowatt-hour. Here’s who pays for it, and the six lines of fine print that decide whether it’s them or you.

The decision it serves: commenting on a large-load tariff, and knowing what to ask at your co-op’s annual meeting. About 15 minutes to read, plus 10 for the Go deeper rungs.

Wires earn a return on capital, which rewards building. A large-load tariff decides who pays if the building outlasts the customer.

Rung 1. Somebody always pays for the substation

Module 1 ended with a question: when one giant customer plugs in, who pays for the equipment it needs? This module answers it.

A data center can’t just plug into the line on your road. It needs its own substation, heavier lines to feed it, and sometimes upgrades farther up the grid. All of that gets built before the customer uses a single kilowatt-hour.

Your co-op or utility pays for that equipment up front, usually with borrowed money, and earns it back a little at a time through monthly bills over many years. If the big customer stays and pays, the plan works. If it leaves early, shrinks, or never shows up at all, the equipment and the loan are still there, and the cost lands on everyone else’s bills. PLAUSIBLE

So the real question isn’t whether a data center pays its power bill. It’s who is on the hook for what was built for it.

AnalogyTen neighbors share a gravel road and split the upkeep. A new neighbor wants the road paved a mile out to his place, on a 20-year loan in everyone’s name. He promises his share will cover it.
Where it breaksIf he moves away, a paved road still helps whoever buys his place. A substation sized for one giant customer may have no second customer in the county big enough to use it.

Rung 2. The large-load tariff: six terms

A tariff is a utility’s published rulebook: the rates and conditions for each kind of customer. A large-load tariff is the chapter written for the biggest customers. It sets what they pay and the conditions for connecting to the grid (Columbia Law School, Sabin Center, June 2026). VERIFIED

As of May 2026, 23 states had approved at least one large-load tariff, and 7 more had one pending (Edison Electric Institute, as cited by the Sabin Center). VERIFIED

The good ones share six terms. Each one closes a different way the cost could slide onto you.

1. A separate rate class

Big customers are billed in their own group, so their costs don’t blend into the rates for homes and farms. Ohio’s regulators ordered the utility AEP Ohio to create a data center class for new loads above 25 megawatts (Public Utilities Commission of Ohio order of July 9, 2025, as reported by POWER magazine). VERIFIED

2. A minimum bill

The customer pays for most of the capacity it reserved, whether it uses it or not. In Ohio that floor is as high as 85% of the capacity the data center signed up for (POWER magazine, July 2025). VERIFIED Reserve 100 megawatts, use 40, and the bill is still figured on 85.

3. Collateral

The customer posts money or a bank guarantee that the utility keeps if the customer fails. Dominion Energy’s tariff in Virginia asks for $1.5 million for each megawatt of capacity (Sabin Center, June 2026). VERIFIED

4. An exit fee

Leaving early costs money. In Ohio a data center can’t buy its way out at all in the early years, and when it can, the fee equals three years of minimum charges (Vorys law firm summary of the order, July 2025). VERIFIED

5. A long contract

The contract should run closer to the life of the equipment. Ohio’s runs up to 12 years, counting a ramp-up period of up to four. Dominion’s minimum is 14. And they’re getting longer: tariffs proposed before 2025 averaged a minimum of about 5 years, and those proposed since average about 12 (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory and the Brattle Group, as reported by Utility Dive, Sept. 2026). VERIFIED

6. Upgrades paid up front

The customer pays for its own substation and lines before construction, instead of the utility borrowing for them. Rappahannock Electric Cooperative in Virginia collects about 60% of a substation’s projected cost within about ten business days of signing (CoBank, Jan. 2026). VERIFIED

Do these terms change anything? After Ohio’s tariff took effect, AEP Ohio’s forecast of large-load demand reportedly fell by about half (Sabin Center, June 2026). PLAUSIBLE as a reported figure. The likely reason: projects that were only testing the water left the line once a real commitment was required.

Field Test: what one dishwasher cycle costs, twiceA minimum bill is about one idea: power costs more at some hours than others, and reserved capacity costs money even when it sits unused. See the first half at home. If your dishwasher plugs into an outlet you can reach, plug a watt meter between them, reset it, and run one normal cycle. Read the kilowatt-hours. Then find two numbers on your co-op’s rate sheet: its on-peak and off-peak price per kilowatt-hour, if it offers a time-of-use rate. Multiply. Say the cycle used 1.2 kilowatt-hours, and your rates were 16 cents on-peak and 8 cents off-peak: that’s 19 cents at suppertime and 10 cents at bedtime, for the same clean dishes. Those are example numbers, not yours. Hardwired dishwasher, or the plug is out of reach? Use the washing machine instead. Plug-in meters only. Never open an appliance, an outlet, or a panel.

Rung 3. Where you get a say

Who writes the tariff depends on who owns the wires (Module 1, Rung 4).

If you’re on a co-op, the board you elect sets the terms, sometimes with a state commission’s approval. Co-ops are already acting. In South Carolina, Central Electric Power Cooperative, which supplies 19 local co-ops, adopted a rate for loads of 20 megawatts or more: contracts of at least 15 years with penalties for leaving early, cash deposits or letters of credit, monthly minimums, and advance payment for upgrades (NRECA RE Magazine, July 2026). VERIFIED In Arizona, regulators voted unanimously on August 12, 2026, to approve a process for the state’s wholesale co-op and five local co-ops. Every large-load agreement gets checked to make sure no other group of customers subsidizes it (Arizona Corporation Commission, Aug. 2026). VERIFIED

If you’re on an investor-owned utility, the state utility commission decides, in a public case that takes written comments. Few local voices show up. The Sabin Center reports that no city took part in Pennsylvania’s large-load proceeding (June 2026). PLAUSIBLE as a reported account. A county or town that files a comment is often the only local government in the record.

Where the critics are right. A tariff protects you from the cost of wires built for one customer. It doesn’t cover everything. A large new load also buys power from the same regional market you do, and where supply is tight, that can raise the price for everyone. Studies find those effects concentrated in tight markets such as PJM, the grid operator for much of the East (E&E News, in the Straight Talk library). VERIFIED Rung 5 explains why. A good tariff is necessary. It isn’t the whole answer.

The first three rungs give you the six terms and where to ask for them. Rung 4. How a rate gets built

Rates aren’t picked. They’re worked out in three steps, and each step is a place where costs can be pointed at one group of customers or another.

Step one: how much money in total

The utility adds up what it needs to collect in a year. That total is the revenue requirement: operating costs, plus the return on what it has built (Module 1, Rung 4). For a co-op, it’s operating costs plus loan payments and a margin the lenders require.

Step two: who caused which costs

A cost-of-service study splits that total among groups of customers, called rate classes: residential, commercial, industrial, and so on. It works in three moves. First, sort every cost by job: making power, moving it long distance, delivering it locally, serving customers. Second, label each cost by what drives it: the amount of energy used, the size of the peak, or simply the number of customers. Third, hand each class its share (South Carolina Office of Regulatory Staff, Electric Ratemaking Fundamentals, Aug. 2025). VERIFIED

The idea underneath is cost causation: whoever causes a cost should pay it. A separate rate class for large loads is cost causation made visible. Without it, a substation built for one customer sits in a shared pool, and the study spreads it across everyone in the class.

Step three: how the bill is shaped

Last, each class’s share is turned into prices. Most bills have two or three parts: a fixed customer charge, an energy charge per kilowatt-hour, and for larger customers a demand charge based on their highest draw (South Carolina Office of Regulatory Staff, Aug. 2025). VERIFIED A minimum bill is a floor under the demand charge. That’s the 85% in Ohio.

Why a big customer can push your rate either way

Here’s the part both sides of the argument get half right. Much of a utility’s cost is fixed. Spread a fixed cost over more kilowatt-hours and the price per kilowatt-hour falls. A study led by the Electric Power Research Institute found that from 2015 to 2024, a doubling of data center capacity lowered residential prices by about 4%. VERIFIED But a big customer also triggers new costs. A Wharton and Harvard Business School paper attributes about 4% of the rise in residential prices from 2020 to 2025 to data centers, and 15% of the rise in industrial prices. VERIFIED Both are in the Straight Talk library.

Those findings don’t contradict each other. Which way your bill moves depends on whether the new customer pays more than the new costs it causes. PLAUSIBLE The six terms are how you make sure it does.

AnalogyA big table at a restaurant can split the check evenly or by what each person ordered. Cost causation is splitting by what you ordered. A large-load tariff makes the guest who ordered the steak pay for the steak, and leave a deposit in case he walks out.
Where it breaksAt a restaurant you can see who ordered what. On a grid, one substation, one line, and one power plant serve many customers at once, so the split depends on formulas that reasonable people argue over. That argument is the rate case.

Rung 5. Why a tariff can’t cover everything

A tariff governs the deal between one utility and one customer. The price of the power itself is set somewhere else, and by location.

Power has a price at every point on the map

In regions with a wholesale market, the grid operator posts a price at thousands of points on the grid. It’s called the locational marginal price, and it has three parts: the cost of making the power, the cost of congestion, and the cost of power lost as heat on the lines (PJM Interconnection, Jan. 2025). VERIFIED

Congestion is the one to watch. When demand is low, power flows freely and prices are close to the same everywhere. As demand climbs, some lines reach the limit of what they can safely carry. The cheapest power can’t get through, so the operator runs costlier plants on the near side of the bottleneck, and the price there goes up (PJM Interconnection, Jan. 2025). VERIFIED

Now put a very large load on the crowded side of a bottleneck. It pays the higher price, and so does every other buyer at that spot, including your co-op’s power supplier. PLAUSIBLE No clause in the data center’s contract with its utility changes that, because the price comes from the market, not the contract.

How big the requests are

Scale is what makes this matter. Rappahannock Electric Cooperative’s ordinary peak demand is about 1,150 megawatts. The requests it has received from data centers add up to about 20,000 megawatts, roughly 17 times its peak, with about a fifth of that under contract (CoBank, Jan. 2026). VERIFIED The co-op buys the power for those loads through a separate affiliate, so the supply costs stay off its existing members’ bills (CoBank, Jan. 2026). VERIFIED That’s a seventh term some co-ops now add: a separate power supply.

Bring your own power

A newer tool goes after the supply side directly. A clean transition tariff makes the large customer responsible for bringing new generation onto the grid to match its load, the way a long-term power contract would. A proposed agreement between Google and Xcel Energy in Minnesota runs 15 years and includes a minimum monthly bill, credit support, and an exit fee (Sabin Center, June 2026). VERIFIED

AnalogyPJM itself compares location pricing to a taxi fare. In light traffic the fare is steady. In heavy traffic the same trip costs more.
Where it breaksA taxi fare goes up only for the rider in the cab. On the grid, when one very large rider crowds the road, the fare goes up for everyone traveling that road at that hour.
Still open SPECULATIVEIs a 12-year contract long enough for equipment built to last decades? The thresholds in today’s tariffs run from under 1 megawatt to 150, so there’s no agreed line for what counts as “large.” And nobody has shown yet whether tariffs written in 2025 and 2026 will hold when the first big customer actually walks away. Questions for researchers, and for your board.

Straight Talk

“Data centers are why my power bill went up.”

Overstated, with a real local risk.

The two best studies so far point in different directions and both find small effects: residential prices about 4% lower with a doubling of data center capacity from 2015 to 2024 (Electric Power Research Institute), and about 4% of the 2020 to 2025 residential price rise attributed to data centers (Wharton and Harvard Business School). VERIFIED Most of what raised bills in those years was something else.

The risk is real where supply is tight and where no tariff is in place. What happens next depends on the rules written now. PLAUSIBLE

Ask for it in writing: a large-load tariff with a separate rate class, a minimum bill, collateral, an exit fee, a long contract, and upgrades paid up front. Read the full entry.

What to ask at your annual meeting

This is the part that reaches your kitchen table: the monthly bill. Co-op annual meetings run from February into spring, and members elect the board there. You don’t need to be an engineer to ask three questions.

  • Does our co-op have a large-load tariff or policy? May I have a copy?
  • If a large customer leaves early, who pays for the substation and lines built for it?
  • Has anyone asked to connect a load of 20 megawatts or more, and what share of our peak would it be?

One thing people get wrong: “Co-ops have to take any customer.” Partly true. In Virginia, state law gives a co-op an obligation to serve, even where the community opposes the project (CoBank, Jan. 2026). VERIFIED But a duty to serve is not a duty to serve at everyone else’s expense. The co-op sets the terms. That’s what the tariff is for. The rule differs by state, so ask your co-op which one applies to you.

What happens if the data center leaves?

“Depends what got signed before the first pole went in. Let’s trace the wire. The co-op built a substation for that customer, and there’s a loan on it. If the contract has a minimum bill and an exit fee, the customer keeps paying or pays to get out, and if it goes broke there’s collateral to draw on. If none of that’s in there, the loan doesn’t leave with them. It stays with the members. So the time to ask is before the vote, not after. Ask for it in writing.”
