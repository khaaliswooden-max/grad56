The Three Clocks: five questions

Answer from memory first. Four right is a pass, and you can retake it as often as you like. Sign in to save your score toward the Field Certificate.

  1. QUESTION 1What are the three clocks?

    Why: Demand for computing moves at chip speed. Power plants, substations, and lines move at grid speed. The third clock is the county’s own calendar of meetings, hearings, and votes, and it’s the only one you set.

  2. QUESTION 2Why is the gap between the chip clock and the grid clock where a community bargains?

    Why: A project on the chip clock wants power sooner than the grid clock delivers it. New power projects wait more than five years to connect (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, July 2026). Whoever needs speed has reasons to accept terms: paying for upgrades, flexing at peak, enforceable benefits.

  3. QUESTION 3Match the chain to its ask. Which ask belongs to the capital chain?

    Why: The capital chain asks who pays for the substation if the customer shrinks or leaves. A large-load tariff keeps that cost with the customer who caused it. As of May 2026, 23 states had approved at least one (Columbia Sabin Center).

  4. QUESTION 4The 2028 forecast for data centers’ share of U.S. electricity runs from 6.7% to 12%. What should a county plan do with that?

    Why: Forecasts differ by nearly a factor of two (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Dec. 2024). If the boom continues, the risk is strain; if it ends, the risk is being left with the bill. Terms that protect you in every case beat a bet on one.

  5. QUESTION 5According to the module, when is the best time to write a zoning ordinance for data centers?

    Why: A county with its rules on paper negotiates on its own clock. An ordinance written before any application is ordinary lawmaking; one written after can look aimed at a single company. We grade that reasoning PLAUSIBLE; ask your county attorney.

Answer all five questions first.
