Small Is Possible: five questions

Answer from memory first. Four right is a pass, and you can retake it as often as you like. Sign in to save your score toward the Field Certificate.

  1. QUESTION 1Who ends up in computing’s “slow lane”?

    Why: Thompson and Spanuth (2021) describe a fast lane for users big enough to justify specialized chips and a slow lane for everyone else, including users whose demand is small or scattered. That describes most rural public services.

  2. QUESTION 2How did rural America get electricity when power companies weren’t interested?

    Why: About nine in ten rural homes had no electric service in the mid-1930s. Farmer-owned co-ops borrowed through a federal program and built the lines; by 1953 more than 90% of farms had power (NRECA). Nobody made the farms bigger. They pooled.

  3. QUESTION 3Which of these is a reason to keep a computing service close to home?

    Why: Big sites win on cost and efficiency. Close wins on four things: fast response, less data to send, privacy, and riding through an outage (Satyanarayanan, 2017). A small site pays for presence, not scale.

  4. QUESTION 4According to the module, what is the scarcest thing a small computing site needs?

    Why: The hard part isn’t the machine. It’s the technician. One county can rarely afford the staff; several member organizations sharing them might. That is the main thing pooling buys.

  5. QUESTION 5In the dishwasher Field Test, what changes between 1 p.m. and 8 p.m.?

    Why: The cycle uses about the same energy either way, roughly 1.1 kWh for a standard ENERGY STAR model. What changes is the mix of plants making power that hour. Jobs that can wait, for dishes or for computers, can be moved to the hour that suits the grid.
