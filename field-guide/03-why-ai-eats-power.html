Why AI Eats Power

Somebody will hand your county a number: megawatts, homes powered, a percent of the grid. Here’s why AI uses the power it does, and how to tell a solid number from a soft one.

The decision it serves: reading an energy claim critically, whether it comes from a developer, a utility, or a headline. About 15 minutes to read, plus 10 for the Go deeper rungs.

Carry this

Chips are limited by heat, and moving data costs more than math.

Rung 1. A computer is a heater that thinks

Inside every computer chip are billions of tiny switches. They have no moving parts; electricity flips them. A switch that’s on stands for 1, and a switch that’s off stands for 0. Everything a computer does, from a text message to an AI answer, is those switches flipping in a pattern, in step with a clock that ticks billions of times a second.

Every flip moves a tiny bit of electric charge, and moving charge takes energy. That energy doesn’t vanish. It turns into heat. One flip makes almost none. Billions of flips, billions of times a second, make a chip you can’t hold your finger on.

So here’s the first thing to carry into any hearing: nearly all the electricity that goes into a computer comes back out as heat. PLAUSIBLE as plain physics; energy has to go somewhere, and a computer doesn’t lift, light, or move anything. A building full of computers is a building full of heaters, and that’s why Module 4 is about cooling.

AnalogyA computer is an electric space heater that does arithmetic on the way. Plug in 1,000 watts of computers and you’ve plugged in a 1,000-watt heater.
Where it breaksA space heater is supposed to get hot. A chip is ruined by heat, so every watt it makes has to be carried away at once, and that carrying takes more electricity.

Rung 2. Voltage is the master dial

What decides how much power a chip burns? Two dials matter most: how fast the clock ticks, and how hard the electricity is pushed, which is the voltage. A chip’s power use rises in step with its clock speed, and with its voltage squared (National Research Council, 2011). VERIFIED

The squaring is the whole story. To run a clock faster, a chip needs a little more voltage. Push a clock 60% faster with about 12% more voltage, and the power doesn’t go up 60%. It doubles: 1.125 × 1.125 × 1.6 is about 2. VERIFIED by arithmetic.

For decades, chipmakers dodged that bill. Each time they made switches smaller, they lowered the voltage too, so chips got faster without getting hotter. Around 2004 that stopped working: voltage couldn’t go lower, and faster clocks meant more heat than a chip could shed. Before 2004, processor performance grew about 100 times per decade. After, a single processor was forecast to improve only about 2 times per decade (National Research Council, 2011). VERIFIED

The industry’s answer was to stop making one fast worker and start putting many workers on each chip. That’s where AI chips come from: thousands of small workers side by side, all doing simple math at once.

You already own proof that heat is the limit. When a processor gets too hot, it slows its own clock to protect itself. Intel calls it throttling (Intel, reviewed Apr. 2026). VERIFIED Your phone does the same thing on a hot dashboard.

Field Test: watch a laptop turn watts into heatCharge a laptop to 100%, then plug its charger into a watt meter. Let it sit idle for five minutes and write down the watts. Point an infrared thermometer at the vent where the warm air comes out and write that down too. Now give it hard work for ten minutes, such as a free benchmark that loads the processor, and read both again. More watts in, hotter air out. Then try the phone version: run a free benchmark app three times in a row and watch the score fall as the phone warms. That’s throttling. The phone reading is PLAUSIBLE, like any phone-app number. Plug-in and handheld meters only. Never open a computer, a charger, or a battery.

Rung 3. Moving data costs more than math

You’d think the arithmetic is what uses the power. It isn’t. Most of the energy goes to fetching the numbers, not adding them.

Stanford engineers worked out the numbers for one generation of chips. Adding two numbers took a sliver of energy. Fetching a number from the computer’s main memory took 500 to 3,500 times as much, depending on the size of the number. In their words, most of the energy goes “not in computation but in moving data to and from memory” (Pedram and colleagues, Stanford University, 2016). VERIFIED

That’s why AI is hungry. An AI model is, at bottom, a gigantic table of numbers, often billions of them. To answer one question, the chip has to pull a great many of those numbers out of memory, multiply, and put results back, over and over. It’s a moving job more than a math job. PLAUSIBLE

AnalogyA cook making supper. Chopping is fast. Walking to the cellar for each onion is what takes the evening. So good kitchens keep a pantry by the stove, and good chips keep small, fast memory right next to the workers.
Where it breaksA pantry can be made bigger. Memory close to the processor is small because closeness is the scarce thing, and an AI model is far too big to fit there. Most of it stays in the cellar.

Two facts that are both true

Each AI answer is getting cheaper. Google reports that a typical text question to its Gemini assistant used about 0.24 watt-hours in May 2025, about what a television uses in nine seconds, and that the figure fell 33-fold in a year (Google, Aug. 2025). PLAUSIBLE: it’s the company’s own measurement and hasn’t been checked by an outside party.

The total is going up anyway. U.S. data centers used 58 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2014 and 176 in 2023, about three times as much, with the growth since 2017 driven largely by AI servers (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Dec. 2024). VERIFIED

Where the critics are right. A company that tells you its chips are getting more efficient is telling the truth and leaving something out. Cheaper answers mean more questions get asked, and longer ones. Efficiency per answer and total power are different numbers. When someone gives you one, ask for the other.

Rung 4. Train once, answer forever

AI uses power in two phases. Training is building the model: feeding it text or images for weeks on thousands of chips until its table of numbers is tuned. Inference is using it: answering one question at a time, for millions of people, for years.

Which one uses more?

Training makes the headlines, but the evidence points the other way. One estimate puts the training of a single large model in 2025 at about 0.31 terawatt-hours, roughly 0.2% of the 155 terawatt-hours that AI-focused data centers used worldwide that year. The same review concludes it’s likely that demand is dominated by inference, not training (Our World in Data, July 2026, citing Epoch AI and the International Energy Agency). PLAUSIBLE: these are estimates, and the companies publish little.

That matters at the county line. A training site can sit almost anywhere there’s power, and can sometimes pause. Answering questions for the public has to happen right now, all day, and often closer to the people asking.

Why bigger keeps winning

In 2020, researchers found that an AI model’s error falls along a predictable curve, called a power law, as you give it more of three things: a bigger model, more data, and more computing (Kaplan and colleagues, 2020). VERIFIED On a curve like that, each further step of improvement costs a multiple of the last one, not a fixed amount more. PLAUSIBLE That’s the business reason behind the building boom: as long as that curve holds, more computing buys a better product.

Not every question costs the same

A short question is cheap. A long one isn’t. Estimates gathered in the same review run from about 0.3 watt-hours for a typical question, to about 2.5 for one with 7,500 words of input, to about 50 for a request where the AI works through many steps on its own (Our World in Data, July 2026, citing Epoch AI and the International Energy Agency). PLAUSIBLE So “energy per question” depends heavily on which question.

AnalogyTraining is building the grain elevator: a big job, done once. Inference is running it every day of every harvest for thirty years. Ask which one moves more grain.
Where it breaksAn elevator lasts decades. An AI model is replaced by a bigger one within a year or two, so the building never really stops.

Rung 5. How to read a power number

Every figure you’ll be shown has soft spots in the same four places.

Power or energy?

Megawatts measure power: how hard something pulls at one moment. Megawatt-hours measure energy: how much it used over time. A site that pulls 100 megawatts around the clock uses 100 × 8,760 hours, or 876,000 megawatt-hours, a year. Substations are sized for the megawatts. Bills and fuel follow the megawatt-hours. A claim that mixes them up, or gives only one, is incomplete.

Nameplate or real?

The number in an application is usually the most a site could ever draw. Real use runs lower. Silicon Valley Power, a city-owned utility in California, reports that its data centers’ peak loads average only about 67% of their nameplate, the rated maximum. Sites also take several years to ramp up to full operation (Energy and Environmental Economics, Dec. 2025). VERIFIED

Firm or hopeful?

Requests aren’t projects. After Ohio required data centers to pay for 85% of the capacity they asked for, requests to AEP Ohio fell from 30 gigawatts to 13 (Energy and Environmental Economics, Dec. 2025). VERIFIED Module 2 covers that tariff. Some developers file the same project with several utilities, which inflates the apparent demand. Ask what share of a forecast is under signed contract.

One number or a range?

Honest forecasts come as ranges. Berkeley Lab’s estimate for U.S. data centers in 2028 runs from 325 to 580 terawatt-hours, or 6.7% to 12% of the country’s electricity (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Dec. 2024). VERIFIED The top is nearly double the bottom, and that’s the experts being candid. History cuts both ways, too: from 2010 to 2018, the world’s data centers did about 6.5 times the computing on only about 6% more electricity (Masanet and colleagues, 2020, in the Straight Talk library). VERIFIED AI broke that balance, and nobody knows whether it’s broken for good.

Still open SPECULATIVEWill cheaper answers keep being swamped by more questions, or does demand level off? Will the curve that rewards bigger models keep holding? Could smaller models running close to home do most everyday jobs for a fraction of the power? Nobody knows yet. Which is the reason to plan in ranges and write contracts that hold up at either end.

Straight Talk

“Data center power use will double forever.”

Plan in ranges.

From 2010 to 2018, global data center electricity rose only about 6% while computing grew about 6.5 times (Masanet and colleagues, 2020). AI broke that balance after 2017, and forecasts for 2028 now span nearly a factor of two (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory). VERIFIED

So “it will double forever” and “efficiency will take care of it” are both guesses. The record supports neither one alone.

Ask for it in writing: low, base, and high scenarios in any utility plan, and what gets built under each. Read the full entry.

What one question costs at your house

People ask whether using AI at home is running up the nation’s power bill. Use Google’s own figure from August 2025: 0.24 watt-hours for a typical text question. PLAUSIBLE A hundred questions a day for a year comes to about 9 kilowatt-hours. At an example rate of 15 cents a kilowatt-hour, that’s about $1.30 of electricity a year.

So your own questions aren’t the issue. The issue is hundreds of millions of people asking, plus longer and harder requests, all served from buildings that cluster in a few places. That’s a question about where those buildings go and who pays for their wires, which is to say, it’s a local question. Yours.

Why does my phone get warm?

“Same reason a wire does. Electricity doing work leaves heat behind. That phone has billions of little switches in it, and every time one flips, it gives off a speck of heat. Ask it to do something hard and they’re all flipping at once. When it gets too warm it slows itself down on purpose, so it doesn’t cook. Now picture a building with thousands upon thousands of those chips running flat out, day and night. That’s a data center. The question isn’t whether it makes heat. It’s where the heat goes, and what it takes to move it.”
