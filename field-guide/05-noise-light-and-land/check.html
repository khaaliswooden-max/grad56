Noise, Light, and Land: five questions

Answer from memory first. Four right is a pass, and you can retake it as often as you like. Sign in to save your score toward the Field Certificate.

  1. QUESTION 1Out in the open, what happens to a sound’s level each time you double your distance from a single source?

    Why: About 6 decibels per doubling, for one source with nothing reflecting the sound (Simon Fraser University). The first doublings are short walks; the later ones are long, which is why equipment placement matters.

  2. QUESTION 2Where should a noise limit for a data center be measured?

    Why: A reading next to a machine says nothing about a neighbor’s house. Rules in Sanford, North Carolina, and Henry County, Virginia, set a decibel limit at the edge of the property.

  3. QUESTION 3Why might a dBA-only sound study understate a data center’s hum?

    Why: A-weighting follows the ear and discounts the low end. A thorough study adds C-weighting (dBC) or looks at each band of pitch (City of Linn Valley).

  4. QUESTION 4What does a pre-construction baseline sound reading give a county?

    Why: Rules that say “no louder than it was before” only work if someone measured before. It’s the cheapest study there is, and it can’t be done after the site is built.

  5. QUESTION 5Federal air rules let an emergency generator run up to 100 hours a year for testing. What do they NOT set?

    Why: The federal rule is about air pollution, not noise or timing. A county can limit test hours itself; Henry County, Virginia, allows routine testing only on weekdays, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.
