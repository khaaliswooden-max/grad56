Ask in writing

  1. What is the decibel limit, where is it measured, and is it an average or a maximum?
  2. Who pays for independent sound testing, how often, and what happens after a complaint?
  3. Where will the cooling equipment and generators sit, and when may generators be tested?

Why these terms

  • A number and a place. Readings next to machines don’t establish the level at a property line or a home (City of Linn Valley, Kansas, updated Aug. 2026). VERIFIED A limit measured at the property line is one a neighbor can check.
  • Examples in force. Henry County, Virginia: 50 dBA at the property line, a 1,000-foot setback from non-industrial land, routine generator testing on weekdays from 9 to 5, testing every year, and money set aside for decommissioning (WDBJ, Aug. 2026). VERIFIED Sanford, North Carolina: 65 dBA at the perimeter, with third-party testing (NC Newsline, July 2026). VERIFIED
  • Spencer County, Indiana. The examples marked “text” come from Ordinance No. 2026-07 as posted by the county. The copy we opened was unsigned, so treat it as drafted language. VERIFIED as the posted text.
  • Low-pitched sound. A-weighted meters are less sensitive to low frequencies, so a thorough study adds dBC or frequency bands (City of Linn Valley). VERIFIED
  • The baseline. A “no louder than before” rule needs a “before.” It can only be measured ahead of construction.
  • Generators. Federal air rules allow emergency engines up to 100 hours a year of maintenance and testing and don’t say when (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Sept. 2013). VERIFIED The hours are a local choice.

This kit is general education, not legal advice. The examples show what other places wrote; they aren’t recommendations. Spot an error? Email ruralamerica56@gmail.com. We correct within 48 hours, in public.
