Straight Talk · #5 · What people say

“They're 96 decibels, 24 hours a day.”

Our verdict

Overstated as a blanket claim; noise complaints are real.

Readings next to machines aren't levels at a home. But neighbors have sued over round-the-clock noise, and some cities now cap it at the property line.

What the evidence shows

  • VERIFIEDReadings taken near machines don't tell you the sound level at a property line or a home. A blanket claim of “96 decibels, 24 hours a day” is misleading; a sustained 96 dBA at a residence would be unmistakably loud and potentially hazardous.

    Source: City of Linn Valley, Kansas, noise and infrasound FAQ (updated Aug. 25, 2026)

  • VERIFIEDNeighbors in Vineland, New Jersey, have sued over round-the-clock noise from a nearby data center campus.

    Source: GadgetReview (Aug. 28, 2026)

  • VERIFIEDThe City of Sanford, North Carolina, changed its development rules in April 2026 so data centers may not exceed 65 dBA at the edge of their property.

    Source: NC Newsline (July 15, 2026)
Where the critics are right

Noise is a real problem for some neighbors, enough to bring a lawsuit in Vineland. And a standard decibel (dBA) reading can understate low-frequency sound (Linn Valley FAQ, above). VERIFIED

What to ask for, in writing

  • A dBA limit at the property line, written into the ordinance.
  • Low-frequency sound measured, not just dBA.
  • Setbacks, and limits on generator test hours.
  • Independent, third-party monitoring.
